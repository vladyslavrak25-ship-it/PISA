Two independent studies, the same result

Study 1.111-country EU report

For Estonia, Czechia and Poland there are two separate studies. They used different subjects (math and science), yet the gap and the share of variance are almost the same. That makes the numbers more trustworthy.

What the gap is made of: family or school

Children from richer families more often attend “richer” schools. So the gap between Q4 and Q1 has two parts: the family’s own status (a student with higher ESCS in any school) and the school (the average status of its students). Study 1.1 estimated both effects, and from them we worked out how many points belong to each.

How this is calculated

For each country, Study 1.1 gives two standardized estimates from one regression: the effect of the student’s own ESCS (β_own) and the effect of the school’s average ESCS (β_school). It also gives the share of ESCS variation that lies between schools (ICC of ESCS) and the gap in standard deviations (d). From these:

SD in points = gap ÷ d
own part = 2 × β_own × 1.271 × SD
school part = 2 × β_school × 1.271 × √ICC(ESCS) × SD
remainder = observed gap − own − school

The number 1.271 is the mean of the bottom quarter of a standard normal distribution; we assume ESCS is roughly normally distributed. The two parts add up to the real gaps within 3–9 points, so the split is plausible. But it is our own estimate built on Study 1.1’s data; the study itself does not contain this split. Everything below is association, not proven cause.

What this means. School accounts for about 80% of the gap in Poland, 57–58% in Czechia and Brazil, and only 31% in Estonia (the rest is the family’s own status and a small remainder). Study 1.1 calls this a “composition effect”, because measured school resources add only 1–4 percentage points to the explained differences.

Why the Ukrainian value is most likely a lower bound. In Study 1.3, students with no data on parents’ occupation (22.7% of the sample) averaged 385.8 points versus 444.3 for the rest, and those with no ESCS (7%) averaged 366.0 versus 436.0. They were excluded from the estimates, so inequality is understated. Study 1.4 adds that 17 of 27 regions are in the sample and frontline regions are excluded; it expects this to understate the link as well.

What we still don’t know. Study 1.1 states that Ukraine is not in the public PISA 2025 file. Study 1.3 itself says it does not know the cycle of its file, and 1.4 calls the same 7,105 students “PISA 2025”. Until this is resolved, read the Ukrainian dot with caution.

What the studies have in common

Six texts by different authors, but together they tell one story.

One question, different theories

Studies 1.1, 1.3, 1.5 and the 11-country report test Bourdieu’s idea and conflict theory: schools reproduce inequality. Study 1.2 looks from the functionalist side: effort and ambition may offset it. Study 1.4 is a research plan for four theories, with no results.

School composition matters more than money

Studies 1.1 and 1.5 independently reached the same point: school material resources barely explain differences. In 1.1 they add 1–4 percentage points to R². In Georgia (1.5) resource shortages are not linked to scores (r = −0.03). The 11-country report adds that the advantage of selective schools shrinks by 12–65 points after accounting for student and school status.

1.11.511-country report

Selection doesn’t work everywhere

In the 11 EU countries, children with higher ESCS consistently get into selective schools more often. In Georgia (1.5), selection by achievement does not explain the result (6.95 points, p = 0.21); there inequality runs through segregation by place of residence and school fees.

Ukraine: everyone says “lower bound”

Studies 1.3 and 1.4 independently conclude that 62.2 points is an underestimate: because of missing parent data and excluded regions.

1.31.4

Ambition: a strong link, but be careful

Study 1.2 finds a 70-point difference (490 vs 420) between students with the same parental education who do and do not want higher education, and r = 0.32. That is more than the entire Q4−Q1 gap in Ukraine (62), so part of the link is probably reverse: stronger students dream of more.

1.21.3

All the studies are honest about limits

Every study notes: PISA is a snapshot in time, so only associations, not causes; it is a test of 15-year-olds, not the NMT (Ukraine’s national exam); and Study 1.3 has no replicate weights, so its intervals are too narrow.

Where the studies disagree

  • Ukraine and the cycle. Study 1.1 says Ukraine is not in the PISA 2025 file. Studies 1.3 and 1.4 have a Ukrainian file of 7,105 students; 1.3 does not know the cycle, 1.4 calls it PISA 2025 and adds 294 schools and 17 regions, which cannot be checked against 1.3.
  • Georgia — a different year. Study 1.5 uses PISA 2022, the rest use 2025.
  • Different subjects. Studies 1.1 and 1.3 are math; the 11-country report and 1.2 are science.
  • “Resilient” students. In Study 1.1 these are poor students who reached level 5+ (Estonia 4.2%, Czechia 2.5%, Poland 3.3%). The 11-country report uses a different definition: 11.7%, 10.4% and 12.1% for the same countries. The numbers are not comparable.
  • “School is 3–4 times stronger.” That is what Study 1.1’s summary says, but by its own table it is 3.1× for Czechia, 2.8× for Brazil and 12× for Poland (0.555 vs 0.045).
